Good morning Brad, this is your Modern Battlefield brief for Friday, November twentieth.

Here's the top line. Overnight, Russia answered Ukraine's strike on the Kerch bridge with one of the biggest salvos of the war: up to eighty cruise missiles, up to twenty ballistic missiles and about four hundred drones, with ballistic missiles going after the bridges in Kyiv. The drones that locked down Royal Air Force Lakenheath last week were most likely three small private planes, which shows how hard it still is to tell what is in the sky over a base. And the Federal Communications Commission did vote yesterday: drone-detection radar now has a legal frequency, and drone control links have a dedicated nationwide band.

The retaliation salvo.

On Wednesday evening Putin promised that Russia's answer to the Kerch bridge strike would be, in his words, far more painful. It came at about one twenty-five this morning. Ballistic missiles hit Kyiv and damaged three bridges over the Dnipro: the Podilsko-Voskresenskyi crossing, the Darnytskyi bridge and the Southern bridge. The Southern bridge is down to one direction with a ten-tonne limit. Cruise missiles, some fired from Tu-160 bombers, hit power plants in the west, and the Lviv, Ivano-Frankivsk and Zakarpattia regions are under emergency blackouts. Two people were killed at a Lviv railway station. A drone hit a hospital in Kharkiv and injured four. The prime minister's count is eight killed and twenty-eight injured nationwide, and the numbers are still moving. About a hundred and twenty of the drones were jet-powered. The Air Force spokesman says the interception rate was "quite high" but has not given numbers yet.

Here's the hard part. Kyiv's defenders have to handle jet drones, cruise missiles and ballistic missiles in the same hour, and only a few interceptor types can stop the ballistic ones. Those are exactly the interceptors the world is shortest of, which I'll come back to. There's also a quieter problem. When the bridges are damaged, a city's own river becomes a barrier. Ambulances, repair crews and fuel trucks face the same weight limits and closures as everyone else, on the morning they are needed most.

One more threat item. Russian channels claim Russia has started using its own interceptor drones against Ukraine's long-range drones. One is called Upyr, and supposedly it got four kills in four flights, hand-flown into slow Ukrainian drones twenty to thirty kilometers out, one of them carrying a land mine as a makeshift warhead. Another, called Zastupnik, is launched from a container. These are Russian claims and unverified. But if they're true, the cheap-interceptor fight now cuts both ways. Ukraine's slow propeller strike drones become the easy targets. And a hand-flown interceptor needs a trained pilot for every kill, so the number of operators caps how many either side can put up.

Detection: Lakenheath, the FCC, and bases at home.

You'll remember the lockdown at Lakenheath on November thirteenth, when people inside the base were told to shelter. A former British Army pilot named Sam Rutherford now tells the BBC that the "drones" were his formation of three Cirrus light aircraft crossing Norfolk at about six thousand feet. "It was definitely us," he said. Only one of the three planes had its transponder on, which he says is normal in tight formation, so public flight trackers showed a single aircraft. Two days later, Moldova's aviation authority said three "drones" filmed over its capital were also three of those planes, having trouble with jammed satellite navigation. The Air Force hasn't responded to his account. Britain's Ministry of Defence logged three hundred and forty drone incidents at military sites in the year to August.

Why is this so hard? Base security forces have to decide within minutes whether to send people to shelters or to try to take something down. Often they are working from reports of lights in the sky and patchy transponder data. A small plane and a drone can look alike on a camera, and even on some radars. Planes in formation can legally show one transponder for the group. Jamming confuses everyone, including the pilots. Every false alarm costs readiness and makes the next real report easier to dismiss, and a missed real one could cost lives.

Now the FCC outcome. At its meeting yesterday, the commission voted to let ground-based radars use a slice of the twenty-four gigahertz band to detect and track drones near airports, borders, prisons and stadiums. It also converted the four hundred fifty megahertz aviation band, which had been voice-only, into a nationwide data network for drone and air-taxi control links, licensed to one company, Aura Network Systems. The final text wasn't out last night, so the details still come from October's draft, including the line that a radio license gives no authority to take a drone down. Here's what's still unsolved. Police and site operators now have a lawful frequency for their radars. But who may actually stop a drone hasn't changed: it is still a small pool of certified federal agents and police officers. And under the coverage rules, the licensee can choose an option that leaves coverage at four hundred feet optional, and that is the altitude where small drones actually fly.

One more base-defense item. The Army has installed its overseas base-security network, called Integrated Base Defense Kitting, at its first installation inside the United States, with more to follow. It ties perimeter sensors and warning devices into one watch floor. The officer in charge put it this way: "We cannot project power forward if we do not protect the force at home." The catch is that home bases now take on the same around-the-clock staffing, maintenance and false-alarm burden that Lakenheath just showed, plus federal limits on what they can do past their own fence.

Counter-drone: a laser meets fog.

A Ukrainian company called Carmine Sky says its four-kilowatt laser, named Blysk, went on duty in Kyiv this month. They claim it burns through a small drone in a fraction of a second and a Shahed in several seconds, out to about a kilometer. In its first days it made no engagements at all, because of fog and low cloud. Lasers promise almost no cost per shot, but physics limits them most in the weather an attacker can choose to fly in. Fog and cloud scatter the beam. A Shahed needs several seconds of steady aim, and a jet drone crosses a one-kilometer zone in a few seconds. Northern European winters are full of that weather. Carmine Sky is also one of about a hundred private companies in Ukraine's air-defense experiment, under Air Force command. That raises its own open questions about command, liability, and who decides to fire over a city.

Protecting the roads.

Ukraine's Defense Ministry says it has now put anti-drone netting over more than two thousand two hundred kilometers of front-line roads. In February, the target was four thousand kilometers more by the end of this year. That's roughly halfway, with six weeks left. Netting works and it's cheap, but it is slow, manual work, and the crews putting it up and patching it work in the same drone-covered zone the nets protect. Russian drones hunt repair crews. Nets sag under snow and tear after strikes. And longer-range fiber-optic drones can wait for vehicles beyond the last netted stretch.

Casualty care: what a drone evacuation actually looks like.

The news agency AFP gave the most detailed account yet of an evacuation by the StarGate heavy drone. A soldier named Yevgen lost his foot to a mine and waited two days on the front line. Russian attack drones forced one launch to be cancelled and a second to be aborted. On the third try, Russian jamming cut the pilot's link mid-flight, and the drone's onboard autonomy kept flying until the link came back. A Ukrainian officer said they need more cases before judging it. The hard part is that even a working evacuation drone doesn't end the wait. The launch site, the route and the pickup all have to be clear at the same time. Until they are, someone near the front has to keep a man with a traumatic amputation alive for two days. That puts the problem back on prolonged field care and on the medics doing it.

Supply: limited by time, not money.

Several stories yesterday point the same way. China now requires export licenses for drone jet engines, larger drone motors, powerful radio modules, speed controllers and some autopilots. It is not a ban, and navigation modules and cameras aren't covered. Ukraine's sanctions envoy called it a start. A Center for Strategic and International Studies update says the US used between a third and two-thirds of key munitions in the thirty-nine-day Iran campaign. It rates Patriot, THAAD and Standard Missile interceptors as heavily used with no substitutes, and it says the problem is not money but time. The Government Accountability Office says the Pentagon has no overarching plan linking munitions production to requirements. In Germany, Hensoldt is handing production of its drone-defense radars to the car-parts maker ZF to keep up with demand. For base defenders, all of this means they cannot count on resupply of the best interceptors. They have to make do with what is already on the shelf.

AI: one operator, many machines.

Finally, the Pentagon says its Autonomous Vehicle Orchestrator challenge is moving forward. The goal is software that lets one operator task a mixed fleet of drones and robots from different makers. It's hard because each machine speaks its own protocol and fails differently when its link is jammed. If the shared network fails, a soldier ends up holding a pile of separate controllers. That's the same operator bottleneck as Russia's hand-flown interceptors.

One thing to think about.

Go back to Lakenheath. Picture a security forces flight chief at dusk. A villager reports three lights. One small plane shows up on the tracker. The radar returns are ambiguous, satellite navigation might be jammed, and he has a few minutes to decide whether to put thousands of people into shelters. The FCC can license his radar and the Army can network his sensors, but neither tells him what the lights are. Identification is the hard part, not detection. It's hard because light aircraft, birds and drones overlap in size, speed and radar return, because the rules let real aircraft fly with transponders off, and because the cost of being wrong is lopsided in both directions. That's the problem sitting with every base defender this winter.

That's your brief. Have a good Friday.
