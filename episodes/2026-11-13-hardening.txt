Hi Brad, this is a Modern Battlefield special. Instead of the news, this one is a deep dive into a single market: physical hardening against drones. That means the nets strung over Ukraine's roads, the tunnels troops are digging to get off the surface, the cages welded onto vehicles and the netting now being specified for American air bases, and the gear an individual soldier carries to stay alive. Along the way I'll tell you who makes this stuff, what it costs, and where I think the money and the openings are.

Here's the top line. First, the biggest physical hardening project on earth right now is Ukraine's road netting program, and it is behind schedule and starting to fail at the margins, which is why units are going underground. Second, this is mostly a low-tech, low-margin, state-and-volunteer-built market, so the value is not in the net itself but in design, installation, maintenance and things built to a real specification. Third, the American version of this market is just waking up: the guidance and the specs exist, but the budget lines mostly don't yet.

Nets over roads.

Start with the roads. In February, Ukraine's Ministry of Defence announced a goal of covering four thousand kilometers of front-line roads with anti-drone protection by the end of twenty twenty-six, and added about one point six billion hryvnias, roughly thirty-seven million dollars, from the state budget for it. The structures are simple: poles or arches on both sides of the road, wire strung between them, and netting draped over the top and sides so a first-person-view drone, an FPV, gets tangled before it reaches a truck. Kharkiv just built its first in-city corridor, six and a half kilometers with nets seven meters high.

The build rate climbed fast. About four kilometers a day in twenty twenty-five, nine kilometers a day by June of this year. By early June they had about eight hundred twenty kilometers, and by early November more than sixteen hundred. But October added fewer than ninety kilometers. So my read is that they will finish the year well under half of the four-thousand-kilometer goal. Most of it is built by a state agency, the State Special Transport Service, not by contractors.

What does it cost? The best number I found is from Kherson, where officials say a kilometer now costs about one point eight million hryvnias, roughly forty thousand dollars, and that price has been rising. The government put about ninety-six million hryvnias into Kherson's priority highways alone. If you multiply forty thousand dollars by four thousand kilometers you get about a hundred sixty million dollars. That's my arithmetic, not an official figure, but it tells you the scale: the biggest anti-drone netting program in the world is a low hundreds of millions of dollars market, not billions.

And a lot of the material is free. The netting supply chain is old fishing nets. A Swedish group called Operation Change says it has shipped about five hundred tons of nets from the Nordic countries. In the US, a recycler called Net Your Problem, working with a small Boston outfit called Ground Squirrel Ventures, has been shipping containers of retired trawl, seine and gill nets out of New Bedford, Oregon and Seattle. They describe an insatiable need for nets. Soldiers even cut gill net into strips and carry them as emergency cover. So the raw material side of this market is donations, which tells you there isn't much margin in selling netting by the ton.

Now the limits. A Ukrainian colonel put it bluntly: a net withstands the first drone. After that it has a hole, and someone has to go out and fix it, every day, under drone observation. Soldiers in the ninety-third Brigade near Kramatorsk say the road nets no longer reliably protect them. Fiber-optic drones, which can't be jammed and fly low, are part of the reason, and volunteers are now building what they call anti-fiber fences to snag the trailing cable. Russia, for its part, builds its own version with poles around fifteen meters tall and fishing line, and sends engineers out to maintain them escorted by soldiers carrying shotguns.

Going underground.

That brings us to tunnels. This month, Colonel Dmytro Pishko of Ukraine's Air Assault Forces described a route of almost six kilometers in Donetsk Oblast connecting two towns. Troops on motorcycles and quad bikes carry food and ammunition in, and wounded out. His line was that the most reliable protection is to dig as deep as possible, and that open trenches are a thing of the past. One honest caveat: in another interview he described it as built of netting, and no depth was given, so it may be a covered or partly sunken route rather than a bored tunnel. The ninety-third Brigade, meanwhile, is using underground passages whose exits come up right at the fighting positions.

The most interesting model for you is on the Kharkiv axis. There, the Ukrainian Association of Developers, which is basically the country's commercial construction industry association, built about two kilometers of connecting routes and twelve underground shelters out of corrugated steel. The association acted as a project office. It took the brigade's requirements, which included ventilation, waterproofing, concealment and the ability to run on its own, then found contractors, compared bids and cut the cost. That is a service business: design, procurement and project management for protected infrastructure, delivered by civilians, for the military.

And it is scaling up to factories. In October, Ukraine's Cabinet changed the rules so private developers can build underground production space with their own or borrowed money, then sell or lease it to defense companies in Ukraine's Defence City program, which already has fifty-one firms and about two billion dollars in revenue. Underground space has become a real estate product.

Russia is doing the same thing. A Ukrainian Marine officer says Russian troops have dug passages up to a hundred and fifty meters long across an estimated twenty to thirty percent of the occupied left bank of the Dnipro in Kherson. Both sides have even sent troops through gas pipelines to get behind the lines. Which creates a mirror market: tunnel detection. A War on the Rocks piece this month argued that quantum magnetometers are the most fieldable quantum sensor for exactly this.

Cages, shelters and active nets.

Next layer up is vehicles and structures. The cope cage, the welded grille over a vehicle, started as a field improvisation and is now a factory product. Russia is offering a factory-fitted kit for the B T R eighty-two A armored personnel carrier: metal grilles, rubber-fabric screens and chains. Reported numbers are about fifteen percent more weight, around sixty man-hours to fit, the turret limited to two hundred seventy degrees, and FPVs set off about thirty-five centimeters from the hull. Those figures come from Russian sources, so treat them carefully. Belarus has a crew-installable mesh kit for its rocket launchers, and Russian shadow-fleet tankers are now showing up with nets and tires to stop sea drones. In the US, the Oklahoma National Guard tested cages and mesh on trucks, a Humvee and an ambulance in September.

The step beyond a passive cage is an active net. Phantom Defense in Ukraine has a system called Mangal that spots an incoming FPV with day and thermal cameras and fires a net in about a fifth of a second, at roughly two thousand hryvnias, about fifty dollars, a shot, by the developer's claim. A volunteer group has built a similar ring of net mortars called DRONOLAW. And the Israeli company ParaZero just got its fourth order for net-firing pods on a European prime's armored vehicle. That's where the vehicle-protection money is moving: from welded steel, which anyone can make, to sensors plus effectors, which not everyone can.

For American bases, the key document is a three-page guide from the Pentagon's counter-drone task force, JIATF four oh one, issued in January. It calls the approach Harden, Obscure, Perimeter, and it names nets and tensioned cables as cost-effective hardening. Langley and Seymour Johnson Air Force bases had already asked industry about netting. And responses were due today on a Tyndall Air Force Base request for retractable anti-drone netting around six open-sided F-35 shelters, each about six hundred thirty feet long and thirty feet high. The spec is specific: mesh no bigger than three inches, two hundred pounds of tensile strength, stops the smallest class of drones, survives four hundred degrees Fahrenheit and Miami-Dade hurricane wind loads, and the sides have to retract quickly so jets can get out. It's market research only, with no promise of a contract. But the Air Force's General Wilsbach put the economics simply: a net that stops a few-hundred-dollar drone from hitting a two-billion-dollar aircraft.

At the heavy end, the Hudson Institute priced sixty hardened tanker shelters at about three billion dollars over ten years, and the Air Force has an open small-business research topic for mobile 3D concrete printers to make blast-shaped shelters, closing in January. So the base market runs from cheap netting to very expensive concrete, with almost nothing funded in between yet.

The individual soldier.

Now down to the person. There are four kinds of things a soldier carries against drones: something to hide, something to jam, something to detect, and something to shoot.

To hide, there are thermal cloaks. A Ukrainian company, STG Defence, makes one called the Chuhaistyr, metallized nylon with silver crystals, developed with Ukraine's military supply directorate. Western equivalents run as much as two thousand dollars. Saab launched its own multispectral Barracuda poncho in May. Here's the catch, and it's a useful one: a test group found a four-hundred-dollar blanket hid the wearer from a thermal drone, but a four-dollar emergency blanket showed up as an obvious cold black silhouette. The cloak has to match the background temperature. So quality and test data are the whole product.

To jam, there's soldier and trench electronic warfare. A trench jammer weighs twenty to fifty kilograms plus a generator, and covers maybe two to three hundred meters. Backpack versions run around eight hours on a battery. A Ukrainian firm, BlueBird Tech, publishes prices: its Hrets line runs from about twenty-two hundred to about six thousand dollars a unit, six to twelve kilograms, with claimed ranges of seventy to four hundred meters. The problem is that fiber-optic drones ignore all of it.

To detect, there are new soldier-worn sensors. The Danish company MyDefence has a passive radio-frequency detector under a kilogram, and the Dutch military is testing a helmet microphone array with a vibrating belt that tells the soldier which direction a drone is coming from. Acoustic matters because it can hear the fiber-optic drones that radio detectors miss.

And to shoot, the shotgun is back. The US Army spent about eleven point six million dollars on more Mossberg five ninety A one pump shotguns, firing tungsten birdshot, for counter-drone use. Beretta showed a counter-drone version of its twelve-oh-one shotgun at the Army's big October trade show, claiming kills out to a hundred meters with special ammunition, and Benelli has its own. Ukraine bought four thousand cheap shotguns at four to five hundred dollars each. There's even a sixty-seven-dollar single-shot net gun sold to civilians. But here's the practitioner view: Ukrainian soldiers told reporters that ordinary rifle rounds, which reach a hundred to a hundred fifty meters, work better than special fragmenting rounds, and that shotguns are mostly for vehicle crews.

How big is this market?.

Honestly, nobody has a good number. The only published figure I found for an "anti-drone net market" comes from a paid research teaser claiming about ninety-three million dollars in twenty twenty-five, growing to two hundred fifty-eight million by twenty thirty-two. But that number lumps in net guns and interceptor drones, and its company list looks careless, so I wouldn't put it in a pitch deck.

Better to build it from the bottom. Ukraine's road netting: maybe a hundred sixty million dollars for the full program. A hardened aircraft shelter: thirty to a hundred fifty million each. A camouflage net: France paid about twenty million euros for three thousand from Saab, so roughly sixty-seven hundred euros each. A thermal cloak: four hundred to two thousand dollars. An EW backpack: two to six thousand. A shotgun: under a thousand. Multiply those by the number of bases, vehicles and soldiers in a NATO army and you can get to real money, but it's spread across hundreds of small buys.

What this means for you.

Here's the one thing worth thinking about. The physical layer is cheap to make and hard to keep working. Nets get holed, cages add weight, cloaks fail if they're the wrong material, and fiber-optic drones beat the jammers. The Ukrainians who are winning at this aren't net manufacturers. They're the developers' association running projects for brigades, and the transport agency fixing kilometers of netting every day. In the US, the bases now have a doctrine that says harden, obscure, perimeter, and a contracting office at Tyndall writing specs, but the counter-drone task force is telling everyone to define the threat before they buy. That sounds to me like an opening for a service company that assesses a site, designs the protection to a written spec, installs it, and keeps it working, priced per protected asset rather than per kilometer of net.
