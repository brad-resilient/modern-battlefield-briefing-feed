Good morning Brad, this is your Modern Battlefield brief for Saturday, October thirty-first.

Here's the top line. Russia's jet-powered Shahed drones went from about a third of a raid to three out of every four in a single night, and the death toll from Thursday's strike on the Dnipro rail platform has risen to fifteen. In the Black Sea, the container ship hit by sea drones earlier this week has sunk, even though a French patrol plane warned Bulgaria's navy in time. And behind the fighting, the money is moving into engines and bases: a Czech company is doubling small jet engine output in Georgia, and the Pentagon says it will harden US bases with private partners rather than new money from Congress.

On Starshield, today is the deadline for Elon Musk to answer lawmakers' questions, and no reply has surfaced yet.

The threat picture.

Start with the numbers from Thursday night. Ukraine's Air Force says Russia launched one hundred thirteen Shahed-type drones, and eighty-three of them were the jet-powered kind. The night before, it was forty jets out of one hundred twelve. Earlier in the week, sixty-nine out of two hundred eighty. So the jet share has jumped from roughly a quarter to roughly three-quarters in a few days. Ukraine did stop seventy-one of the eighty-three jets, about eighty-six percent, which is better than the sixty percent average we heard about on Wednesday. But one night is not a trend, and the gun crews we heard from on Monday get five to twenty seconds against a jet.

The cost in people keeps climbing. The Dnipro platform toll is now at least fifteen dead. Later that day another drone set a vocational school dormitory on fire, and about three hundred fifty thousand people in the region lost power. The same day, drones hit an apartment building in Kremenchuk, killing at least three, and cruise missiles hit central Chernihiv. Twenty-seven people were killed across Ukraine in one day.

Now a story from the other side of the drone equation. Defense News got inside France's first dedicated drone-combat unit. A Marine infantry regiment took one of its armored car squadrons, the crews who used to fire a cannon, and turned them into the First Hunter-Drone Squadron: about a hundred drone pilots in a unit of a hundred twenty, with surveillance drones, two platoons of first-person-view attack drones, and a loitering munition platoon. In an exercise in Estonia, about ten French pilots, roughly five percent of their battlegroup, caused twenty percent of the losses inflicted on a US opposing force riding in Bradleys, in one day. In another exercise they took out four regimental command posts, plus supply convoys and air-defense units.

The honest numbers are worth hearing too. New pilots hit five to ten percent of the time; experienced ones about thirty percent, against better than ninety percent for the cannon they gave up. One pilot can go through ten to twenty drones in a day. The regiment wants eighty to ninety percent hits using autonomous final guidance, with mother drones directing smaller ones launched from canisters. France plans about fifteen of these units by summer twenty twenty-seven. The officers admit their opponents had weak counter-drone defenses. That's the point for us: command posts and convoys die first, and a US unit without real counter-drone cover looks a lot like that opposing force.

On Iran, here's what changed. On Thursday a small boat placed two explosive charges on the hull of a Dubai-owned tanker in the Strait of Hormuz and started an engine room fire. The Financial Times says they were limpet mines, the first successful use since twenty nineteen. There were twenty-eight attacks on shipping in and around the strait in October alone. The Financial Times also reports a planned US assault of about three days on nuclear sites, regime targets and power plants, possibly starting the week after Tuesday's midterm elections. A carrier group and an amphibious group with about ten thousand sailors and Marines arrive by the end of November, and another Patriot battery is headed to Israel. Officials haven't confirmed the plan. If it happens, expect renewed drone and missile fire at Gulf bases and infrastructure.

Detection and early warning.

Two items. First, a Ukrainian think tank called BRDO and a European-Ukrainian defense tech alliance called New Age Defense launched a Drone Security Index. It scores a country out of one hundred on four equal parts: prevention, detection, response and recovery. They count one hundred twenty-nine drone incidents in eighteen European Union countries since twenty twenty-two, with Germany second only to Romania. Pilot assessments of Germany and Poland start in November. The same day, the European Union named two drone and counter-drone technology hubs, in Latvia and the Netherlands, opening in twenty twenty-seven. Scorecards like this become buyers' requirements, and Ukrainian groups are positioning as both assessors and suppliers. Note that recovery, meaning repair, backup power and casualty handling, counts as much as sensors.

Second, the commander of US Forces Korea, General Xavier Brunson, said this week that his forces face jamming every day, including GPS jamming in the West Sea. His line: anything that depends on clean satellite links, unlimited bandwidth or unbroken fiber "will not survive forty-eight hours on the peninsula." He's asking industry for light, jam-resistant radios that are hard to detect, and sensors with open interfaces. It's the Starshield lesson from a US four-star: any warning network has to keep working jammed and offline.

Counter-drone.

The US military trained a joint class of counter-drone operators in Germany on a system called RD-SUADS, from Trust Automation, bought through Joint Interagency Task Force four-oh-one. It's a single container that fits a standard air pallet and can be airdropped, with a radar mast covering all directions, a camera, jamming antennas, and a built-in generator that runs four days without base power. No test results were released. It's a drop-in box for defending a command post or a small site. The catch is that it relies on jamming, and as we heard this week, Russia's drones are adding compasses and camera navigation to fly through jamming.

Ukraine is attacking the cost side. Its Brave1 defense tech cluster opened grant competitions for air-to-air missiles, domestic radars and ways to defeat glide bombs, with the stated aim of cutting the cost of shooting down jet drones. A grant round for jet engines comes next.

Protected movement.

The Contship Jet, the container ship hit by three sea drones off Bulgaria on Wednesday, has sunk. All eighteen crew were rescued. Video from the deck shows a black drone boat, about five or six meters long, running alongside before turning into the hull. Analysts think it's a modified Russian design, which isn't confirmed. Here's the key detail: Bulgaria's navy commander said a French patrol aircraft warned them of the threat, and they still couldn't stop it. At least six civilian ships were hit by sea drones off Bulgaria in October. Earlier this week the Black Sea countries chose early warning over escorts. This is the first public test of that choice, and warning alone failed. Zelensky is in Istanbul today to talk Black Sea security with Turkey's president.

Hardening.

On Tuesday we reported that the Pentagon's FORTRESS America base plan reportedly had a ninety-day standard. Now an assistant secretary, Dale Marks, has confirmed it: a mandated ninety-day stockpile on each base of critical parts, fuel, propellant and ammunition, backed by 3D printing depots that make spare parts on site, plus a goal of thirty to ninety days of power without the civilian grid. The catch is money. Marks said it will rely on public-private partnerships, not going back to Congress "with my hand out," and there's no deadline for every base. So base hardening now has a standard but not a budget, which favors companies that can finance shared infrastructure like power plants, fuel storage and printing depots.

And a small research item: Army labs tested camouflage coatings in Greenland that could change with season and light, like an Arctic fox. The hard part is that snow looks white to your eye but different through other sensors, especially thermal. Next comes a vehicle test in Alaska under thermal cameras, and they cite thousand-dollar drones as the reason.

Casualty care.

Navy corpsman Petty Officer Second Class Jajuan Hernandez, attached to the Marine Raiders, received the Marine Corps' Angels of the Battlefield award this week for a night in March near Erbil, Iraq. A one-way attack drone detonated about twenty-five meters from his position. As the only American medic there, Hernandez ran into a burning sleeping area while ammunition was cooking off and treated seven critically wounded French soldiers. That meant tourniquets, chest seals, IVs, keeping them warm, whole blood transfused straight into the bone, needle decompression of collapsed lungs, and opening chests to relieve pressure, all across a language barrier. The Marine Corps credits that care with saving six lives. One drone made a mass casualty event that one medic had to handle alone, and the thing that made the difference was whole blood ready in the field and the training to use it.

Money.

Two items. Czech company PBS Group is putting another hundred million dollars into its plant in Roswell, Georgia, to build up to ten thousand small jet engines a year by twenty twenty-eight, nearly double today. PBS also agreed to set up licensed engine assembly in Ukraine. That's the third engine signal this week, after China put these engines under export license and a Dutch company offered China-free ones. Small jet engines power cheap missiles, jet drones and jet interceptors alike, and the supply is becoming its own investment category.

And an analysis worth reading from War on the Rocks. An industry insider argues defense tech has split into two markets: companies with production contracts, and companies living on prototypes. About forty-nine billion dollars of venture money went into defense tech last year, but the top hundred startups won only about four point three billion in awards. The author names counter-drone, cheap attack drones and generic artificial intelligence as overcrowded, and expects quiet failures and more mergers.

The one thing to think about.

This week, warning kept arriving on time and people still died: ten minutes in Dnipro, a French plane's alert in the Black Sea. The crowded money is going into sensors and counter-drone gear. The thin part is what happens after the warning: the shelter a few steps from where people wait, the defense on the ship itself, the medic with blood in the bag. Ask yourself which of those you could build in the next year. That's the thing worth thinking about today.
