Good morning Brad, this is your Modern Battlefield brief for Tuesday, November tenth.

Here's the top line. Washington is turning homeland counter-drone into a process and a market: five departments agreed yesterday on one way to vet counter-drone systems, and the Homeland Security secretary says the customer is anyplace that has a fence. The Pentagon is now lending money to build the drone supply chain itself, with a four hundred and fifty million dollar loan to Neros, while the Army says its real shortage is trained operators, not drones. And the cheap close-in layer keeps getting formalized, from an American interceptor-drone test in Poland to Belgium rebuilding air defense from scratch, even as Russia turns its Shahed-type drones into small cruise missiles.

Homeland counter-drone.

Start at home. Yesterday the Departments of Transportation, War, Homeland Security, Energy and Justice signed a memorandum, led by a White House task force on airspace security, that sets one process for evaluating counter-drone systems before any of them deploys one. The Federal Aviation Administration and the Federal Communications Commission share requirements and test data along the way. The aviation agency says it has already approved more than a thousand counter-drone coordination requests, and for systems it has already assessed, a review could take as little as twenty-four hours. That matters because of what happened in February, when El Paso's airport was shut down after a laser counter-drone system was fired without a finished safety review.

At a grant forum near Washington the same day, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin put the cost problem bluntly: a forty-thousand-dollar missile against a twenty-dollar drone. He said stadiums weren't built for counter-drone gear, talked up small autonomous interceptors he called killer bees, and said the future buyers are data centers, universities and, quote, anyplace that has a fence. He also floated leasing, so cities and counties don't have to buy systems outright. The emergency management agency's chief told local agencies to use the grant money now. The administration cites five hundred million dollars committed, half of it for the World Cup.

Two cautions. The memorandum brings no new money and no new legal authority, and a grant does not give a police department the right to bring a drone down. Why this matters: the bottleneck at home is approval and authority, not hardware. A system that clears this process once can be deployed far faster after that, so vendors with clean test data for aviation and spectrum regulators get the edge. And leasing or managed service for stadiums, campuses and cities is an open business model that the secretary named himself.

Cheap interceptors, American style.

Overseas, an American test battery is doing what Ukraine has been doing for a year. From November second to sixth, at the Ustka range on Poland's Baltic coast, a battery from the Fifth Battalion, Fourth Air Defense Artillery, ran an exercise called Audacious Fury. They flew the FD-1, a first-person-view interceptor drone made by Mountain Horse Solutions, against small and medium drones and massed attacks. The maker says it's ready to launch in under two minutes. It can be flown by hand or cued by radar, and a human approves every intercept. The Army has ordered it twice this year, in the spring and again in October, through its tactical acquisition directorate, though it hasn't released quantities, prices or results.

The interesting part is the job this battery has. It is writing the tactics for teaching air defenders with no drone experience to fly interceptors, and then it will train other units. Why this matters: this is how a Ukrainian improvisation becomes American doctrine, and then a requirement with money behind it. The gaps it exposes are training people to fly these things, handing a radar track to a cheap shooter fast enough, and proving they work.

Belgium shows the other end of the scale. It scrapped all of its ground-based air defense after the Berlin Wall fell. Yesterday it reactivated its fifty-first Air Defense Battalion after thirty-seven years. The package it calls Blue Dome includes ten NASAMS missile launchers, twenty Rheinmetall Skyranger gun systems with thirty-millimeter cannons for drone swarms, fourteen radars and fifty-four command vehicles. The Skyrangers sit on truck-loadable platforms to guard fixed sites, at a cost of several hundred million euros, but deliveries don't start until late twenty twenty-eight. And in the Gulf, an undisclosed country is buying a sixteen million dollar package of sensors, soft-kill effectors and command software from Australia's Electro Optic Systems to protect strategic sites. The point: Europe is buying the right layer, but it arrives in two years. Bases and infrastructure need something in between, and fast, cheap, leasable point defense plus the software that ties sensors and shooters together is what sells in that gap.

The threat: a cruise missile at drone prices.

Here's what those defenses face. Ukraine's military intelligence examined a nearly intact Russian drone called the Geran-5 K5-K and says it is effectively a small cruise missile. It's about four meters long, carries a ninety-kilogram warhead, thermobaric on the one they found, cruises above four hundred and fifty kilometers an hour with bursts near a thousand, and flies about a thousand kilometers. It navigates with an anti-jam satellite antenna copied from a Chinese design, plus the camera system called Susanin that matches the ground below to stored pictures, so jamming GPS doesn't blind it. Of ninety-three foreign parts inside, fifty-two came from American makers like Texas Instruments and Analog Devices. These are Ukrainian intelligence claims, without a count of how many have been used. Why this matters: this is the third step in a pattern since October, first jet speed, then navigation that ignores jamming, now cruise-missile performance at drone cost. Point defense has to assume the threat arrives fast and isn't fooled by GPS denial.

On the electronic front, Ukraine's General Staff says it hit a Russian station built to jam satellite communications near Sartana in occupied Donetsk. Ukrainian media call it a Starlink jammer. That's a claim, without imagery. The same day Ukraine's parliament backed creating a Space Forces branch, meant for missile early warning and secure satellite links, after October's Starshield restrictions. The lesson: anything at the front that relies on a commercial satellite link needs a fallback, because those links are now jammed and the jammers are hunted.

Bridges and terminal seekers.

Movement next. Kyiv's two main bridges over the Dnipro are being hit by ballistic missiles. On November seventh six Iskanders targeted the Northern Bridge and four hit, and it's closed both ways. The Southern Bridge was damaged in late October. Serhii Beskrestnov, a well-known Ukrainian radio expert, wrote on Telegram yesterday that jamming doesn't save bridges, because the Iskander's seekers, optical or radar, scan the ground in the final seconds and correct course. That's one expert's analysis. Ukraine plans twenty-one alternative river crossings, the first in Kyiv in early twenty twenty-seven, and Zelensky has asked for free surface transport during closures. Why this matters: fixed crossings can't be protected by electronic defense alone. That leaves interceptors, decoys built to fool image and radar matching, and redundant crossings with fast repair, a capability Western engineer units are thin on.

Shelters and rescuers.

The Komyshuvakha story we first covered last Friday has grown worse. The underground school hit by a three-ton bomb on November second was a heated community hub where people came to eat, warm up and charge phones. Police have now recovered fifty-five bodies and say nineteen more people may be under the rubble. On Sunday, drones struck the rescuers at the site three times, wounding two drivers and damaging vehicles and two search dogs. The same day, an FPV drone hit a vehicle carrying three energy repair workers in Sumy, one of them critically hurt, and the grid operator says they were deliberately targeted. Kyiv, meanwhile, says only fourteen of the fifty mobile shelters it promised will arrive this year.

Two lessons. A shelter that gathers people is a target when the enemy has a three-ton bomb; it needs dispersal and blast-rated design, not just a basement. And rescue and repair crews now need their own drone warning and protection, the same gap as the eighty percent shortage of armored ambulances we heard about Saturday.

On the care side, a small study in Military Medicine from late October, new to us, interviewed twenty Swedish soldiers back from deployment. They described first aid as judgment, team coordination and mental readiness, not just a checklist, and the authors say it should be trained that way because a medic may not come soon. With Russian casualties waiting fourteen and a half hours for a surgeon, the soldier next to the casualty is the care system.

Money.

Two money stories. The Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital gave Neros, maker of the Archer FPV drone and a frontrunner in Drone Dominance, a four hundred and fifty million dollar conditional loan. Neros adds a hundred and sixty-five million of its own. It has brought motor production in-house, will start making circuit-board components, and aims to build about eighty percent of each drone's value itself by late next year. The loan isn't forgivable, and the government wants Neros to sell parts to other drone makers too. Finland also signed about three hundred million euros with Ukraine yesterday for joint drone and defense production.

And the Army says launched-effects drones, which carry swappable surveillance, jamming or strike payloads, are now in eleven of its twelve division headquarters, the last one next month. Most come from Griffon Aerospace, which can supply hundreds a month and delivered its first batch about three weeks after the Army decided to scale up, through the Defense Department's drone marketplace. But the Army still hasn't put small attack drones in every squad, and back in September it said the reason was too few trained operators.

The signal across both: government money is moving upstream, into components and production, and the marketplace route works for scaling hardware fast. The part nobody has solved at scale is people.

The one thing to think about.

Here's the thread through today. Drones, interceptors and launched effects are all getting cheaper and easier to buy, through marketplaces, loans and fast approvals. What's not keeping pace is the human side: operators to fly the drones, air defenders to fly the interceptors, soldiers trained to keep a casualty alive for hours, and rescue crews who can survive the second strike. If you want a place to build where the big players aren't crowding in, ask who trains, certifies and protects the people who run all this new hardware.

That's your brief. Drive safe.
