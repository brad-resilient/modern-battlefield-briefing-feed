Good morning Brad, this is your Modern Battlefield brief for Wednesday, November eleventh. As you asked, today opens with a deep dive on drone defense as a service. The short version: there is real demand and real money, but in the United States today the business is detection plus paperwork, because only certified police may take a drone down. Europe is starting to let site owners shoot, and that is where a small firm could get in fastest. After that, a shorter news round: US bases in Britain on high alert, a Ukrainian detector that hears Russia's mesh radios, and a survey that says water, not shelling, is what breaks soldiers.

Deep dive: drone defense as a service.

Start with who is actually buying. In the United States, the biggest buyer right now is local police, paid for by the Federal Emergency Management Agency. FEMA's counter-drone grant runs about two hundred fifty million dollars a round, and the second round's state applications are due Monday. Dallas added ten point three million dollars of Dedrone detection to its long Axon contract. Texas state police spent about three point two million on detection and gear that can jam or take over a drone. Delaware County, Pennsylvania, took one point four million for Dedrone sensors, portable kits, training and five years of support. That works out to roughly two hundred eighty thousand dollars a year for a county network. That vote passed only three to two, and the two no votes are worth hearing: privacy, and fear of being locked into a subscription once the grant runs out.

Beyond police, the buyers are utilities, stadiums, prisons, data centers, universities and military bases. The Air Force has already asked for a subscription service at Joint Base Andrews, where the contractor owns and runs the sensors and the base just gets alerts on a dashboard. In Europe, the telecom company Orange launched a subscription detection service in March, putting sensors on its nearly twenty thousand towers and rooftops across France. And in Ukraine, about a hundred companies now pay for their own air-defense crews, with machine-gun turret towers from a firm called Carmine Sky and trained teams from one called Guardia, firing only when the Air Force says so.

Now the legal line, which shapes everything. In the US, federal agencies in defense, energy, justice and homeland security have had authority to defeat drones since twenty eighteen. Last December's defense bill added a law called the Safer Skies Act. It lets state and local police and prison staff detect, seize, disable or destroy threatening drones, but only after certification. Detection certification is an online course. Defeat certification is a two-week residential course at the FBI's counter-drone school in Alabama. Officers must notify the Federal Aviation Administration within five minutes of switching on a jammer, and report within forty-eight hours. By July first, only sixty-one officers in forty-six agencies had been trained, out of roughly seventeen and a half thousand agencies. And here is the key detail for a business: contractors cannot be certified.

Private site owners can watch, record, log, and call the police. They cannot jam, take over or shoot. Even some detection is touchy: sensors that decode a drone's radio link to find the pilot may run into federal wiretap law, according to critics, while radar, cameras and Remote ID receivers are seen as safer. Treat that as a common reading, not legal advice. The FAA has proposed letting infrastructure owners request no-fly zones over their sites, but it isn't final. Senator Tom Cotton has a bill that would let trained staff at critical sites take drones down, with liability protection and two hundred fifty million dollars in grants. It has no cosponsors. And Monday's five-department agreement speeds up equipment approvals, but it grants no new authority.

Europe is moving faster. On Monday, the Czech government approved a draft law that lets trained staff at airports, power plants and other critical sites take down drones inside zones the operator applies for, as long as the operator files a risk assessment, documentation and training records. Parliament still has to pass it. The European Union's drone action plan from February funds detection and test centers but leaves the shoot-down question to each country.

What about price? No one publishes a subscription price list. What's public: the Delaware County deal, the Texas and Dallas grants, and Russian turret tenders at eleven to thirty-six thousand dollars each. DroneShield, the most open of the vendors, says its recurring revenue jumped about two hundred thirty percent in the first half of this year. But that's still under a tenth of its sales, and only fifteen percent of its customers are civilian. So subscription counter-drone is growing fast from a small base.

There is also an insurance angle. In the Baltics, banks now require solar parks to carry terrorism and sabotage cover, after drones hit an Estonian power-plant chimney and a Latvian oil tank. Corporate premiums run to tens of thousands of euros a year. No insurer is yet reported to ask for drone detection. That's an opening, but it's my inference.

So where does a small firm get in? My read, and this part is inference. In the US, be the bridge, not the shooter. Install and run detection the site is clearly allowed to use. Write the site's use and privacy policy. Prepare the year-long standing operations plan the local police agency needs. Handle the FAA no-fly-zone filing when that rule lands. And produce logs an insurer will accept. The site pays a monthly fee, and the certified police agency, funded by those grants, does any take-down. Second, sell speed through vetting: help vendors package test data for the new federal review, or help sites pick gear already approved. Third, in Europe, sell the training and certification package the Czech law requires, and carry it to the next country that copies it. Fourth, short-term leases for big events. The risks are real: grants end, Axon and Shield AI keep buying up the platforms, and the bill that would let private owners shoot isn't moving.

Detection and counter-drone.

Ukraine's company INFOZAHYST says its Pluton passive detector now picks up the mesh radios that let Russia's Geran and Gerbera drones relay signals to each other and shrug off jamming, with a claimed range up to seventy kilometers. That's a vendor claim, but it answers a problem we've tracked since October. In Germany, TYTAN Technologies and KNDS showed an armored pickup that launches interceptor drones, and TYTAN says it plans to build up to three thousand interceptors a month.

Hardening and the rear area.

US bases at Mildenhall and Lakenheath in England went to Force Protection Condition Charlie, the second-highest level, over an unspecified Iranian threat, and thirteen aircraft, including tankers, moved to Scotland, Germany and Spain. The answer, again, is dispersal and tighter gates. Meanwhile Britain says it will send military cyber teams abroad after hackers warmed refrigerators at France's national blood service and in supermarkets. That's the same pattern as the freezer failures at US base commissaries. In Ukraine, a Russian first-person-view drone hit a power repair crew in Sumy, critically injuring one worker. Protecting the site isn't enough when the people who fix it are hunted on the road.

Movement and the soldier.

Ukraine's military ombudsperson surveyed one thousand six hundred sixteen soldiers. The top stressor wasn't shelling. It was lack of water: eighty-three percent called it critical, and three in four cited no resupply. Most soldiers last about a month on position before exhaustion sets in, and the average deployment in complaints to the office was a hundred fifteen days. Water delivery by drone and robot is force protection, not comfort.

The one thing to think about.

If you pursued drone defense as a service, would you rather be the company that owns the sensors, or the company that owns the relationship between the site, the police and the insurer? The second needs less capital and is harder to copy. That's your brief. Have a good day.
