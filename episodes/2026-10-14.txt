Good morning Brad, this is your Modern Battlefield brief for Wednesday, October fourteenth.

Here's the top line. The Army has already written, and field tested, a specification for a battlefield triage wearable, and that may be the most important single document for your thesis we've found so far. Soldiers wounded by an Iranian drone in Kuwait in March told the Associated Press that their injuries were missed and some waited about ten days to be flown out, which is the American version of the delayed evacuation problem. And on day two of AUSA, the Army's big annual conference, the new autonomy command took shape, with the Army's medical research command sitting inside it, while the first Army counter-drone laser got a target date of early twenty twenty-eight.

The triage wearable spec.

Let's start with the big one, because it's squarely in your lane. Earlier this year the Defense Innovation Unit, working with the Army office that buys soldier medical devices, ran something called the AI-Assisted Triage and Treatment Challenge, with a prize pool of just under a million dollars. What they asked for reads like a product requirements document for a Pulse-like device. A portable, networked system that monitors a casualty's circulation, what clinicians call hemodynamic status, and gives early warning of shock and internal bleeding. It has to be small, rugged and light, safe to fly on aircraft, and run at least seventy-two hours without a recharge. It can be worn or work from a distance. It has to be "place and forget," usable by a non-medic. It must log data locally when there's no network and sync fast when one appears, and connect to ATAK, the Android Team Awareness Kit that soldiers use for maps and situational awareness, and to BATDOK, the military's digital casualty record tool. It needs FDA 510(k) clearance for trauma use before it can be fielded, and the vendor has to be able to make fifteen thousand units in the first year.

One hundred ninety-eight companies applied. Six were picked and tested in May during an exercise called Sword twenty twenty-six in Poland, where units from the sixty-eighth Theater Medical Command, the thirtieth Medical Brigade and the second Cavalry Regiment ran a full mass-casualty scenario, judging how well each device caught internal bleeding and unstable circulation. The Army says the results feed its decision on moving vendors into a program of record. The winners weren't publicly named. One participant that did go public is a company called Biostream, which builds an edge-computing wearable with alerts for medics.

Why this matters to you: it's a named buyer, a written spec and a path to a long-term program, all at once. Detecting deterioration from an overdose and detecting deterioration from blood loss are close cousins. Your homework is to find the other five vendors, learn whether prototype contracts followed in June, and ask honestly whether Resilient's FDA path could clear that 510(k) bar.

Combat casualty care.

Next, the Kuwait story. On March first, the second day of the war with Iran, an Iranian drone hit Port Shuaiba in Kuwait, killing six American troops and wounding dozens. Nine of the wounded spoke on the record to the Associated Press, which is rare for soldiers still serving. Their accounts: a lieutenant colonel with a damaged artery in his neck, at risk of stroke, was sent to a barracks and told to book outpatient appointments. Some of the wounded were put up in government employees' homes in Kuwait for about ten days before being flown out. Several were coded as not wounded in combat, which meant they weren't admitted at Landstuhl, the big military hospital in Germany. And several had their traumatic brain injuries diagnosed only after they got home to Fort Hood. One major waited more than four months for MRI results. The Pentagon says the care was appropriate. The Associated Press links the gaps to years of shrinking American medical presence in the Middle East.

This builds on the inspector general report from Monday's brief, where helicopters were turned back and most evacuations went by ground. Here the failure wasn't stopping bleeding, it was recognizing injuries, recording them and tracking people. A wearable that logs blast exposure, the vital-sign trend and the time of injury, and travels with the casualty, speaks to both the missed brain injuries and the paperwork error.

Now some good news on blood. This month in Florida, Hillsborough County Fire Rescue, Tampa General Hospital and a company called Archer First Response Systems are launching drones that lower whole blood on a tether to trauma scenes, in a climate-controlled box. Two drones cover seventy square miles, and the county projects an average delivery of about three minutes, though that's a projection until they fly a real call. Yesterday's Israeli lessons report recommended exactly this, drone delivery of whole blood forward. Heavier casualty drones are moving too. A British firm, Hybrid Drones, was due to start production this month on a quadcopter that can carry four hundred kilograms, about eight hundred eighty pounds, for casualty evacuation in Ukraine. And in May, American cavalry soldiers in Poland strapped a medical dummy onto a heavy-lift drone called the Flowcopter. Civilian emergency medical services could be a real beachhead for you, with real regulators and real outcome data. And notice the missing piece: something has to tell the system which casualty needs that blood first.

Research.

A research paper in the latest issue of the journal Military Medicine backs that point. A team from Boston Medical Center, MIT Lincoln Laboratory and the Joint Trauma System built a tool called Field AI Triage, trained on nearly eight hundred thousand civilian trauma patients and almost nine thousand military ones. Using vital signs alone, its ability to predict who'll need a massive transfusion scored about point six seven on the standard zero-to-one accuracy scale. Adding context, meaning how the person was hurt and where on the body, pushed that to about point eight eight. It's a statistical model on registry data, not yet tested in the field. The lesson for a product: a wearable that only streams heart rate and oxygen will underperform. The value is in fusing vitals with what happened. That argues for a device that also senses or logs the injury event, and those Lincoln Lab and Joint Trauma System researchers are worth knowing.

Drones and the autonomy command.

At AUSA, the Army filled in details on its new Futures and Autonomous Systems Command. A three-star general, not yet named, will lead it. It starts with autonomy at the corps level: autonomous artillery, robotic boats for resupply, armored reconnaissance, obstacle breaching, and helicopters teamed with drones. Fort Rucker becomes the Army's center of excellence for drone autonomy, with fielding aimed at fiscal twenty twenty-eight. Here's the detail that matters to you: the Army's Medical Research and Development Command is one of its subordinate commands. Medical research money now reports up through the autonomy command, so a pitch linking casualty monitoring to robot evacuation and autonomous resupply speaks the new boss's language. A related Army Research Laboratory study warns that soldier trust in robots and degraded communications remain unsolved.

From Ukraine, the commander of its Unmanned Systems Forces, Robert Brovdi, known as Madyar, claimed his drone units killed or wounded nearly twenty-seven hundred Russian troops in the first five days of October, up about forty percent from the same days in September. Those are Ukrainian claims that can't be verified. But the direction is clear: more drone casualties, spread across a wider kill zone, with fewer medics per casualty. Mass-casualty triage is the scaling problem.

Counter-drone.

On defense against drones, the theme is cost per kill. The Army's first enduring high-energy laser, AeroVironment's LOCUST X3 mounted on a Joint Light Tactical Vehicle, under a contract of about four hundred sixty-five million dollars for more than twenty-six units, is aimed at counter-drone operations by early twenty twenty-eight. Anduril unveiled Steelfin, a family of cheaper interceptor missiles it claims can do eighty percent of the Patriot interceptor's job at under half the cost, with flight tests in twenty twenty-seven. Those are company claims. A defense tech Substack newsletter adds a self-funded laser shoot-off in Utah from November thirtieth to December eleventh, with up to two hundred million dollars in follow-on work, plus new cheap interceptors from Raytheon and a startup called CHAOS Industries. And Kongsberg will build a counter-drone factory in Poland. A Seventh Infantry Division officer put it plainly: lasers are one layer, not a solution. Drone casualties aren't going away.

Money.

In money, Anduril won a contract worth up to one point eight billion dollars over five years to field the Army's Next Generation Command and Control system, its battlefield data backbone, starting with the corps in the Pacific. Any battlefield sensor, including a casualty monitor, will eventually need to feed that data layer, so Anduril is becoming a gatekeeper. At AUSA, eight more countries joined the Army's online marketplaces for drones and counter-drone gear, bringing it to twenty-nine. Allies can now buy pre-cleared systems in days instead of months, so a product cleared once can reach many buyers quickly. And quietly, the Army is shifting expendable medical supplies toward smaller, distributed theater depots as it reviews its war stockpiles, which pairs naturally with drone resupply.

The one thing to think about.

Here's the one thing worth thinking about today. Across three days, three separate Army doors have opened for body-worn monitoring: chemical defense with LifeLens, the brain health and blast program, and now the triage challenge run by the soldier medical devices office. The Kuwait story shows the need isn't only stopping bleeding, it's knowing what happened to each soldier and carrying that record forward. So the question is: which door does a Pulse-like device walk through first, and who, by name, is standing behind it?
