Good morning Brad, this is your Modern Battlefield brief for Thursday, November twelfth.

Here's the top line. The Pentagon now treats saying no to counter-drone defense as a risk that has to be written down and reported, while the Air Force shops for simple netting to keep small drones off its F-35s. In the cheap interceptor race, the bottleneck has moved to the seeker and the launcher, and money is starting to follow. And casualty evacuation without a pilot just got a small but real Defense Department contract.

Correction.

First, two corrections to yesterday's deep dive on drone defense as a service. Senator Cotton's bill, S. 4380, would let not just site staff but site owners' contractors be certified to take drones down, with liability protection and two hundred fifty million dollars in grants from twenty twenty-seven through twenty thirty-one; it still has no Senate cosponsors, but that contractor clause matters, because the Safer Skies Act doesn't allow contractors at all. And on the Federal Emergency Management Agency's counter-drone grants, the first two hundred fifty million dollar round went only to World Cup and America two-fifty host states plus the D.C. region, while the second round, due Monday, is the one open to all fifty-six states and territories.

Base defense from the top down.

Now the news. Defense Secretary Hegseth signed a five-page memo on November fifth, and his office put out a corrected copy on Tuesday. The core instruction: if anyone delays or denies a request to use counter-drone systems, that has to be logged as a "risk of inaction" and reported to Joint Interagency Task Force four-oh-one, the Pentagon's counter-drone hub. In the memo's words, protection against drones "will not be delayed by self-imposed restrictions." It calls small drones the defining threat of our time. The correction itself was about lawyers: it moves legal reviews to the task force's own legal adviser instead of a new deputy general counsel. The Pentagon didn't say why.

What this does is flip the burden. Until now, a cautious lawyer or commander could quietly slow things down. Now the slowdown leaves a paper trail. Pair that with Monday's five-department agreement on vetting counter-drone gear, and you can expect approvals at US bases to speed up, with an advantage for systems that come with their legal and spectrum paperwork already done.

At the other end of the scale, the Air Force put out a notice asking industry about netting for six big sunshade shelters at Tyndall Air Force Base in Florida. Each one is six hundred thirty feet long and holds up to six F-35s. The specs are refreshingly concrete: mesh openings no bigger than three inches, strong enough to stop the smallest class of drones, those under twenty pounds, retractable on the ends so jets can taxi in and out, and rated for Miami-Dade hurricane winds. Responses are due tomorrow. No budget yet, and no promise of a contract. The Air Force chief of staff has said light protection is worth it but hardened shelters aren't economical. That's a niche for industrial fabric and tensile-structure companies, and it doesn't need any counter-drone license.

The interceptor stack comes apart.

Next, the Army's mobile answer for protecting maneuver units. Slides posted from an October industry day show the next short-range air defense increment, a self-contained pallet that slides onto a light truck, a utility vehicle or even a ground robot, won't reach its first battalion until late twenty twenty-nine. That's later than the earlier target. It has to handle everything from quadcopters to Shahed-type drones and swarms, run on its own for seventy-two hours, fit in a C-130 and be air-droppable. The Army leaves the choice of sensors and weapons to vendors, and the likely bidders include L3Harris, Leonardo DRS, Moog, SAIC and Sierra Nevada. Remember too that the stopgap budget blocks this as a new start. So for four years, the gap gets filled with kits, interceptor drones and marketplace buys.

Ukraine, meanwhile, is pulling the interceptor apart into pieces, and each piece is becoming a market. A Ukrainian-Lithuanian startup called Valkyrie Dynamics raised one million euros for a radar seeker that weighs about a hundred grams and sits in an interceptor's nose. It guides the drone over the last hundred to five hundred meters, in any weather, and the company says it wants to sell it for under three hundred euros at volume. That's a vendor claim. Why care? Camera-guided interceptors struggle at night, in fog and against decoys, and other interceptor makers have said they're waiting on a seeker. A cheap radar seeker is the part that unlocks mass interception of jet drones.

Former Ukrainian defense minister Mykhailo Fedorov said his new company, Mission Defense Partners, will make cheap anti-drone missiles its top priority, including one with about fifteen kilometers of range and a radar seeker. He also said its factories employ up to a thousand people but run at about thirty percent of capacity, and Palantir's chief executive is reported as the expected first investor. That comes from a single interview, with no dollar figures.

And then there's the launcher. A Ukrainian charity and tech cluster are expanding something called Cradle: weatherproof boxes, each holding an interceptor drone, placed along the routes Shaheds fly. When a target shows up, an operator, possibly hundreds of kilometers away on a fiber-optic line, picks the nearest box and launches in a minute or two. The batteries keep each box ready for a week to ten days, and one operator can run three to six launch stations. The brigade that bought the first ones in January says they haven't failed in ten months. The reason it matters: fast interceptors only reach fifteen to twenty-five kilometers, so you need lots of launch points, and the crews at those points get hunted. Take the crew out of the box and you take them out of the target. The same idea would fit bases, substations and stadiums at home, though US law limits who could press the button. That's where the Cotton bill comes back in.

The threat picture.

The long-range drone war ran hot in both directions this week. Overnight Tuesday into Wednesday, Russia launched a hundred sixty-three drones at Ukraine, and ninety-one of them were jets. That's more than half. It knocked out six big substations, with outages in eight regions. Ukraine hit a Baltic port near Saint Petersburg, about eight hundred fifty kilometers from the border, plus two refineries, one of them in Omsk, roughly two thousand four hundred fifty kilometers away. Russia shut twenty-six airports, including Saint Petersburg's. Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia all put out drone alerts as NATO jets scrambled. Russian military bloggers claim up to three Russian helicopters were lost chasing drones, but their stories conflict and nobody has confirmed it. One more detail from the Institute for the Study of War: Russia is building about eighty-two drone storage structures at an air base in Crimea, ten of them hardened. Both sides now treat their drone fleets as assets worth bunkering.

And a warning about ceasefires. Ukrainian commanders say Russia used the late-October humanitarian ceasefire in the river town of Oleshky, the one meant to evacuate civilians, to move in over a thousand troops, plus drones and artillery. They also say Russia scouted basements as drone-operator hideouts and stored gear in the town hospital. Those are Ukrainian claims that haven't been independently verified, and Russia says there were no violations. Ukraine's negotiator noted that drone surveillance is what made the ceasefire checkable at all. If the Abu Dhabi talks produce more local truces, watching them becomes a sensing job. Meanwhile a Ukrainian commander says Russia drops thirty-five to forty glide bombs a day on river crossings near Kupyansk. Crossings are still where movement dies.

Casualty care and evacuation.

Two items here. US Africa Command gave Merlin Labs five hundred thousand dollars to turn a Cessna Caravan, a rugged single-engine cargo plane, into an autonomous air ambulance. Early in this fiscal year, in Morocco, it's supposed to fly the whole mission on its own: take the tasking, take off, fly to a simulated casualty collection point, load a simulated patient, and fly back and land. There's no remote pilot and no flight crew. A follow-on version carrying several litters is possible. The money is small, but a combatant command running a challenge creates a paper trail toward a requirement. This is the long-distance version of the problem, suited to Africa and the Pacific, not the short drone hops we see in Ukraine. The open slots are the medical module, monitoring the patient in flight, and loading at rough airstrips.

On the research side, a paper in Military Medicine follows a combat-medicine course for Ukrainian frontline medics from twenty twenty-three through last fall: a hundred and four graduates, including ten new instructors. The course moved from procedures like stopping the bleeding toward keeping someone alive for six to seventy-two hours. That means managing cold, blood chemistry and clotting, and tracking trends with nursing checklists. Ukrainian instructors now do most of the teaching. It's a program review, not an outcomes study, but it matches what we saw Monday: Russian casualties averaging about fourteen and a half hours to a surgeon. The training market is shifting from bleeding control to long holds.

Money.

Quickly. The Pentagon gave a California robotics firm called Machina nine million dollars to prove it can build American Shahed-style attack drones in shipping-container robot factories, each cell turning out an airframe in two to five days at an estimated fifteen to forty thousand dollars. Two US sites come first, then possibly forward sites like Kuwait. Bloomberg reports the Ukrainian-founded drone company Uforce is now valued at six billion dollars with backing from Britain's National Wealth Fund. I only saw the headline, so treat that one lightly. And Germany's military said it will stop training with Chinese-made drones and buy European ones instead.

The one thing to think about.

Here's the thread. Yesterday we said the American site owner can buy detection and compliance but not the takedown. Today's correction cracks that open a little: if the Cotton bill ever moves, a contractor could do the takedown too. And Ukraine is showing what that service could look like: remote launch boxes, cheap seekers, a crew nowhere near the target. So the question worth chewing on is this. If you were building a drone defense service for a base, a substation or a stadium, which parts would you build now that are legal today, like detection, netting and paperwork, so that you're ready to add the interceptor the day the law allows it?

That's your brief. Drive safe.
