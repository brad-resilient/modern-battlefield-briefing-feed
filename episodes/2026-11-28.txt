Good morning Brad, this is your Modern Battlefield brief for Saturday, November twenty-eighth.

Ukraine's stop rate against Russia's jet-powered drones bounced back to about seventy-three percent overnight, and the big strike Zelensky warned about hasn't come yet. Kuwait now says the two Italian technicians killed on Thursday died from falling shrapnel while they were maintaining air-defense antennas, and Italy is moving its troops out of Kuwait. And on the medical side, a Navy medical battalion's families say its surgeons have spent nine months deployed with some never operating, while a study of wounded Ukrainian soldiers finds most say they're fine and turn down psychological help.

The air war.

Overnight into Saturday, Russia sent one hundred sixty-two drones at Ukraine, seventy-four of them jet Shaheds, plus decoys. By eight in the morning, Ukraine's Air Force said it had downed or jammed one hundred forty-one, including fifty-four of the jets. That's about seventy-three percent, up from about sixty-one percent the night before, and back in the band of the four nights before that. There were hits at thirteen locations. On Friday, drones hit two schools in central Kremenchuk, hurting four people, three of them children, along with a transport site in Dnipro and rail and government buildings in Korosten. One newsletter tally counts three dead in Nizhyn.

What changed: fewer jets came in, nine fewer than the night before, and the rate recovered. But no missiles came either. Zelensky's warning on Thursday of a new mass strike on energy and logistics hasn't played out yet. One better night doesn't change the trend. Ukrainian analysts say the number to watch is a sustained rate above eighty percent, which would show new interceptors arriving in numbers, and it hasn't been there since mid-November.

The same night went the other way too. Moscow's mayor, Sergei Sobyanin, said four hundred sixty-two Ukrainian drones flew toward the Moscow region, that most were stopped far out, and that one hundred twenty-three were destroyed near the capital. Flights were halted at Vnukovo and Domodedovo airports, with restrictions at four regional airports. Russia released no damage figures. It's the same saturation problem from the other side. Even a capital with layered air defense had to shut its airports for a night.

Kuwait follow-up.

Yesterday's open question was whether the drone hit the Kuwaiti air-defense station directly or whether debris fell on it. Kuwait's Ministry of Defense now says the attack caused shrapnel to fall, and analysts at Critical Threats read the statements as shrapnel from intercepted drones. The two men were Antonio Saccoccio, sixty-five, and Emanuele Lo Russo, thirty-six, working for Rheinmetall Italia under a service contract. They were maintaining the radar and detection antennas of the Skyguard air-defense system. Italy's foreign minister says the drone was made in Iran, while noting that doesn't prove Iran launched it. Iran denies it and has offered to join a fact-finding commission. Kuwait summoned Iran's ambassador, and Italy's defense minister told Parliament that Italian troops in Kuwait will move to Saudi Arabia and other countries.

This confirms the worst case from yesterday. The intercept can work and still kill the people servicing the sensor underneath it. Antenna work happens in the open, at the very site the attacker is aiming for. And the answer here was to move people away, not to protect them where they work.

Casualty care.

Three items today, all about the people who treat the wounded.

First, a report from MS NOW, relayed by other outlets and not independently verified. The Navy's First Medical Battalion got about a week's notice in late February to deploy from Camp Pendleton for three to six months. It's been gone nearly nine. An unnamed officer wrote to his senator in October that operating in field tents, without an armored medical facility, was "an inadequate environment for delivering medical care." Spouses say some surgeons haven't done a single operation in eight months because there was no safe place to operate, and that after a missile struck near one post, injured troops went to a civilian hospital. A military official disputes this, saying every surgical team has an assigned hardened facility and that Central Command has received no requests for more supplies. There are two hard problems in one deployment. Surgery needs a protected room, and a tent isn't one. And surgeons who don't operate for months lose the hands-on skill a mass-casualty day will demand.

Second, an Army field hospital in South Korea wrote up a practice move at night, under radio silence and a simulated drone threat. The one hundred twenty-first Field Hospital moved its emergency room, operating room and intensive care to a nearby facility in the dark. No patient moved until an advance party confirmed the new site, and the old emergency area stayed open until the last vehicle left. The leaders gave section chiefs authority to decide on their own, because, as the executive officer put it, they "did not want leaders waiting on a phone call to make a decision about a patient." And the senior enlisted leader, Master Sergeant Miriam Reyes, said this: "fatigue is a patient safety issue. Protecting their rest must be part of the plan, not an afterthought." The hospital that moves survives, but every move is a sleepless night for the people who have to run an operating room the next morning.

Third, research. A French legal scholar, Marianne Lahana, interviewed twenty-one wounded Ukrainian soldiers and nine health workers this spring and summer, and presented the results in Kyiv. Eighty-one percent of the wounded said they were doing fine psychologically. But almost half showed signs of trauma during the interview itself. Seventy-one percent turned down psychological follow-up or called it pointless. About half would rather talk to fellow soldiers, and only twenty-nine percent were open to professional support. Thirty-seven percent wanted to go back to combat. A soldier who lost a leg to a drone said, "I can only talk to my fellow soldiers, and that's it. Not to psychologists." It's a small study, but the point is clear. The problem isn't only a shortage of psychologists. The people who need help don't trust the help on offer.

Counter-drone.

France launched a counter-drone pact with its defense industry on Friday, running to late summer twenty twenty-seven and covering air, sea and ground drones. The junior armed-forces minister, Alice Rufo, called the wars in Ukraine and the Middle East "a quite brutal reality check," and said French forces have already had problems from not having enough counter-drone capability. Drones have flown over France's nuclear submarine base, the Toulon naval base and an explosives plant. The French procurement agency also says it took delivery this month of its first interceptor drones, the Gobi Zephyr from Harmattan AI, about two months after ordering them. They fly at about three hundred fifty kilometers an hour, kill by ramming, and a human authorizes each mission. No numbers or price were released.

Meanwhile, Israel's defense ministry said a second US-Israeli trial called Black Sands, run with US Central Command and the Pentagon's counter-drone task force, tested about twenty counter-drone systems in Israel. Neither government named the systems or released results, from this round or the first one at Camp Lejeune last year. Buyers are getting faster at ordering, but the evidence of what works stays private, so units that weren't there can't learn from it.

On lasers: Ukraine's defense-tech cluster, Brave1, now says a laser from one of its grant recipients, run by a private air-defense team, shot down a jet drone. That backs the earlier claim, but there's still no date, place, footage or wreckage. An Asia Times analysis lays out the limits: fog and cloud, about a kilometer of range, and several seconds of burn time per jet drone. No results yet from the other new laser, the Tryzub.

Detection.

Early Friday, an unidentified drone slipped into Latvian airspace near the Russian border for a few minutes. Latvia launched an interceptor drone, NATO jets were scrambled, and this time the interceptor came home. In early November one blew itself up after the target crossed back into Russia. But in none of Latvia's four engagements this month is an interceptor reported to have hit anything, and in one case conditions to fire were never met, partly because of risk to civilians. A drone that's inside for a minute or two is gone before anyone can close on it.

Quick follow-ups.

The forty-eight-missile Patriot contract still has no signature reported. Poland's defense minister said the US reaction to the November twentieth missile crash "hasn't been the reaction we were expecting," and the no-fly zone isn't in force yet. China extended its pause on the gallium export ban, but only for sixty-one days, to January twenty-seventh. The Helsinki Commission still hasn't named witnesses for its December third hearing, there are no Mongoose test results, and the FCC's final drone-radar order still isn't posted.

One thing to think about.

Go back to that wounded soldier who said he only talks to his fellow soldiers. Then picture the Navy surgeon who hasn't operated in eight months, and the field-hospital crew that moves all night and then staffs an operating room at dawn. The hard problem is keeping the people in this system whole: rested, skilled and willing to ask for help. It's hard because none of it shows up on a radar screen until it fails. Fatigue builds over months, not nights. A surgeon's skill fades quietly without patients. And the soldiers who most need help are the ones who say they're fine and don't trust the people offering it. Peer support helps, but peers are tired and hurting too. Nobody has worked out how to measure any of this early enough to act, and the drone war keeps adding to the load every night.
